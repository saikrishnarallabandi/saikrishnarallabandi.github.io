Judith answers in my family's group chat.

She is a personal assistant, and she has been running without a break since June 2026 — in WhatsApp threads, in my email, and through a pair of smart glasses. Most assistants wait to be opened. Judith is already in the room, which turns out to be a much harder problem.

Running now Built and run by Sai Krishna Rallabandi

An assistant for one person is a feature. An assistant in a room full of people is a different problem.

Once an agent is in a conversation with other people in it, ordinary assistant questions become questions about those people. Anyone present can try to steer her. She accumulates things she was told in confidence. And an answer that is perfectly correct can still be the wrong thing to say in front of the wrong person.

Group threads

She replies where more than one person can see her, and anyone in the thread can try to redirect her.

Smart glasses

The same agent, speaking only to me. Here she is allowed to start the conversation, because the audience is one person.

Quietly, in the background

Scheduled check-ins, mail, health signals and a job board that keep running while nobody is asking her anything.

Every part of her keeps exactly one promise.

The interesting part of Judith is not the model. It is the layer around it: a set of small, separate components, each responsible for one thing and nothing else. One decides whether an action is safe to take. One decides what is worth remembering. One decides whether what she is about to say is fit to be seen by a person.

They are kept deliberately small, because a promise that small can be checked. When something goes wrong, the answer should be a particular component that made a particular decision — not a shrug and "the model got it wrong."

The names are Sanskrit, and each one is the promise: jataayu, the bird that fought to protect someone in flight, is the guard. Smriti, memory, is what she keeps. Sakshi, the witness, writes down what happened. Drishti, sight, is how I see what she did.

The part I did not expect.

Splitting a system into small promises is supposed to make it easier to tell what broke. Mostly it does. But building it this way also taught me three things I would not have guessed, and they are the reason I keep writing this down.

A promise can be carefully designed, correctly written, deployed, and still never actually take effect — because two components were configured separately and each did exactly what it was told. A promise can quietly stop being kept while every alarm still reports that everything is fine. And a promise nobody wrote a record of cannot be shown to have been kept at all.

None of that is visible in an architecture diagram. It only shows up if you go and read what the running system actually did, which is most of what this project has turned out to be.

The engineering detail is on its own page — the lifecycle of a single message, what a component has to declare, and the full list with what is genuinely running. It is written from the source and reads like a specification, which is the point of keeping it separate.

The same agent, in a room of one.

On the glasses Judith is allowed to speak first, because only one person can hear her. Everything else is unchanged: gather the context, decide whether this moment is worth interrupting, and send the answer only to the person entitled to it. The rule that governs a group thread is the same rule. The room is what changed.
