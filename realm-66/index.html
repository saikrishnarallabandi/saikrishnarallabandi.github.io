What Agent Benchmarks Don’t Tell You: Recoverability and Credit Assignment in Agentic Retrieval Evaluation

Sai Krishna Rallabandi, Parag Pravin Dakle, Preethi Raghavan

Fidelity Investments

Paper (PDF) Poster (PDF) 5-min video REALM accepted papers

The headline number is not a property of the agent. The standard report — how much a tool-using agent beats one-shot RAG on a benchmark — is jointly determined by a property of the benchmark and by which sub-component of the agent happens to be load-bearing. We give a four-stage protocol that separates the two, and apply it to ~6,000 logged agent episodes over seven corpora and three reader families.

Two confounds a single number absorbs

Benchmark selection determines the verdict. Whether extra retrieval can help at all is a property of the task, fixed before any system is run. If the first retrieval misses gold evidence but that evidence is still reachable, more retrieval has a target; if it is not reachable, no amount of agency helps. The usual proxies — “multi-hop,” “hard,” the raw coverage gap — do not separate the two cases.

The gain is credited to the wrong component. An agent changes at least two things at once relative to a one-shot pipeline: it retrieves more than once, and it chooses what to do next. A positive delta is conventionally attributed to agency — but a fixed, choice-free loop that only re-queries may capture the same gain.

The four-stage protocol

  1. Characterize before you measure. Compute an LLM-free recoverability profile for each benchmark before observing any outcome: of the gold evidence the first retrieval misses, what fraction is reachable by query reformulation (Rref), what fraction only by structural navigation (Rnav), and what fraction by neither (absent-from-query). Stratify reporting by regime, not by difficulty label.
  2. Assign credit with an ablation ladder. Report three arms, not two: one-shot RAG (R1), a choice-free fixed-iterative loop (R2), and the full agent (R3). The statistic is the gap-closing fraction γ = (R2 − R1)/(R3 − R1) — the share of the agent’s advantage that iteration alone already delivers. Only the residual 1 − γ is evidence for agency.
  3. Validity checks that can flip a verdict. Pre-registered equivalence testing (TOST) for “no difference” claims, multi-reader replication, metric-artifact audits, and cross-family judge re-grading.
  4. Price the horizon. Report quality against retrieval calls, tokens, and latency on a Pareto plane — the agent’s advantage and its expense grow together.

What the protocol finds

−0.21 to +0.30range of the headline agent gain across corpora, same harness, same readers — the finding is the change of sign
65–96%of the agent’s gain on reformulation-recoverable corpora is captured by the choice-free loop (γ)
+0.15residual agency gain on the longest-horizon, navigation-recoverable corpus (ConditionalQA), under every reader, after iteration saturates by round 2
~6,000logged agent episodes, 7 corpora, 3 reader families (Claude Sonnet, GPT, open Qwen)

The counterexample is the point. ObliQA’s high-gap subsets miss 65–68% of gold evidence — as much as the recoverable Wikipedia subsets — and the agent gains nothing there, because those misses are absent-from-query: no query reformulation can name them. A coverage-only characterization predicts +0.18 for these points; the measured value is ≈ 0. Meanwhile the recoverability profile, computed before any outcome was observed, predicts the sign and rough magnitude of the agent advantage out of sample (Spearman ρ = 0.79 over the non-ObliQA subsets).

And the protocol does not collapse into a blanket negative about agents: on ConditionalQA, where missing evidence must be navigated through document structure rather than rediscovered by a better query, iteration saturates by round 2 and full agency keeps its residual under every reader. An evaluation protocol that can only produce negative results about agency would not be useful. This one localizes where agency actually pays. The paper closes with a reporting checklist (§9) so the measurements become a discipline rather than an anecdote.

Abstract

Agent quality on long-horizon tasks is usually reported as a single number: the aggregate gain of a tool-using agent over a non-agentic baseline on a benchmark. We argue that this number is not a property of the agent. It is jointly determined by a property of the benchmark and by which sub-component of the agent happens to be load-bearing, and reporting it alone makes agent quality look benchmark-dependent and irreproducible. We propose a four-stage evaluation protocol that separates the two. Stage 1 characterizes the benchmark before any outcome is observed, using an LLM-free diagnostic we call recoverability: whether evidence missed on the first retrieval is still reachable from what the system has already seen, and if so whether it is reachable by reformulation or only by structural navigation. Stage 2 assigns credit with an ablation ladder — one-shot RAG, a choice-free fixed-iterative loop, and a full agent — that localizes any measured gain to iteration or to open-ended agency. Stages 3 and 4 add the validity checks and cost accounting that change verdicts in practice. Applying the protocol to about 6,000 logged agent episodes over seven corpora and three reader families, we find that the headline agent gain ranges from −0.21 to +0.30 (each corpus in its native metric) purely as a function of the recoverability regime, and that on reformulation-recoverable corpora 65–96% of the gain normally credited to agency is recovered by the choice-free loop. Agency earns its cost only on the longest-horizon, navigation-recoverable corpus, where iteration saturates by round 2 and leaves a +0.15 residual under every reader. The protocol turns each of these into a measurement rather than an anecdote.
